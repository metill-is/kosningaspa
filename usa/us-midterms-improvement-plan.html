US midterms: what the model and data need next

2 November 2026, the day before the election. Follows the feasibility report on this branch (usa/us-midterms-feasibility.html). Started so far: only the MEDSL Senate and President downloads.

The model has a working national layer and no real race layer, so it can forecast the vote but not seats. Three fixes to the national layer use data we already hold. Forecasting seats needs a race layer built from past results, one file of which only you can download, plus a few choices that are yours to make.

Six decisions and up to three actions are yours. Two decisions run out at 23:00 UTC tomorrow (18:00 ET), when the first polls close: whether to record a second 2026 forecast, and how to prove when our forecasts were made. Everything under “I do now” needs nothing from you.

Pick an option in each, then copy your answers at the end of this section and paste them into the chat.

D1 · Record a second 2026 forecast before the polls close?

Needed by 23:00 UTC on 3 November (18:00 ET).

The 2026 result is the only test that will ever be fully blind. Every change made after it is counted is tuned with the answer known. v4's D+6.5 is already recorded. The question is whether to also record v5, which is v4 plus an offset for registered-voter and adult polls, against likely-voter polls. To keep the choice honest, the test for recording v5 is fixed now: v5 is recorded only if, on the eight backtests, its mean absolute error is no larger than v4's and at least as many of its 90% intervals cover the result.

My pick: b. v5 is needed in every later plan anyway, so the only extra work is writing down its number. Option c would put an untested Senate layer on record and teach us little.

D2 · How do we prove the forecasts were made before the election?

Needed by 23:00 UTC on 3 November, for v4's record as well as any v5 record.

The branch is local and commit dates are whatever the machine says, so today nothing proves that D+6.5 was written before the count.

My pick: a. It keeps the poll margins private, which you asked for, and it needs nothing from you beyond the yes and the owner.

D6 · Machine time on your Mac

Every fit runs locally. Measured on v4: 0.8–8.2 minutes per backtest fit, two at a time; 12 minutes for the 2026 national fit; 23.5 minutes for the full simulated race layer. Each new national variant is 8 backtest fits plus one 2026 fit. Each race-layer variant is 3 election-eve backtests. Your memory notes also list an Icelandic 2024 leave-one-pollster-out backtest that is set up but not run.

My pick: a for tonight, since D1 has a deadline. After that it matters little.

D4 · Which data sources may we use?

The repo is public, so the tables stay outside git, in the study folder, whatever you choose. The question is which sources may feed the model at all. Terms are listed in the data table; “not checked” means I have not read that source's terms.

My pick: a. It keeps publishing possible later. The House forecast needs the presidential vote by district on the 2026 lines. The Downballot is the one source I've found that may have it after the 2025–26 redraws, so its terms are the first thing I'd check.

D3 · What are we building towards?

This sets how far phases 2 and 3 go. The next midterm is 2030. In 2028, a presidential year, the House and a third of the Senate are both up: the national layer works as it is, but the fundamentals differ (no midterm penalty; coattails instead).

My pick: b, deciding on c once the race-layer backtests can be set beside 538's published 2018–2022 numbers. Publishing adds the data-terms and public-code questions, which there is no need to answer yet.

D5 · Expert ratings and fundraising in the race layer?

Silver's Deluxe model blends in Cook, Inside Elections and Sabato ratings, and his fundamentals use fundraising. Both help House races, which have almost no polls. Ratings tie the model to paid or unchecked sources and make it harder to say what the model knows on its own. Fundraising is public FEC data but needs an API key and candidate matching.

My pick: a. It keeps the first race layer reproducible from open data. It also lines up with 538's Classic model, which had no ratings, so that is the fair comparison.

1 · National layer, with data we already hold

I do now (no decision needed)

  • Election-day poll error. Extra variance on the forecast step for poll error the carried bias doesn't capture, its scale set from the backtests one year out at a time. This targets the 2020 interval miss. It is one term in the forecast step.
  • Fundamentals prior. Regress the national House margin on the president's party, previous margin and approval, midterms only, from the 1946–2022 results we hold; fit it leaving out each backtest year, and switch it on in the backtests. Gallup approval is in hand from the American Presidency Project, not yet parsed.
  • Uncontested seats. Measure how much seats with no Democrat or no Republican move the national House vote that anchors the walk, 2018–2024. The 2022 and 2024 lists are in hand; 2018 and 2020 come from Wikipedia. This decides whether the anchor needs adjusting.
  • Partisan sponsors. Give partisan and internal polls a bias prior towards their sponsor. Our generic-ballot tables have no sponsor flag, so I'd take it from 538's poll files where they overlap.

I do after

  • Backtest each change on its own, then together, and update the report's backtest section with v4 against each variant. One commit per change.
