Can the kosningaspá model forecast the US midterms?

Feasibility study · 2 November 2026, the day before the 2026 midterms · branch claude/usa-midterms-model-feasibility-0897e9

Yes: the kosningaspá model can be adapted to the US midterms and run on a laptop. There are enough data for the national vote and the Senate. The poll core carries over, but at US poll density two changes are needed before it samples well: the walk integrated out by a Kalman filter, and each pollster's overdispersion partly centred. Races, fundamentals and seat counts are new. With those changes the national model fits in 12 minutes and a 2026-sized simulated race layer in 24. Refitted with only the data available on the eve of each election from 2018 to 2024, it missed the national House margin by 2.7 points on average, as the polls did, and by 2.4 against their 3.4 once it had an earlier result to learn poll bias from. Six of its eight 90% intervals, on the eve and four weeks out, covered the result. Both misses came in cycles whose poll error grew after the first months, which a bias held fixed per cycle cannot see. The remaining work is mostly data (the race pipeline, fundamentals), plus correlated errors between races and a poll bias that can move within a cycle.

1 · DataYes for the national vote and the Senate, thin for House districts. Four national results since polling archives begin (2018–2024), 4,201 generic-ballot polls from 161 pollsters in the fitted data, Senate polls in 30 of 2026's 35 races. District polls are scarce (≈31 listed for 2026), so House races would rest on presidential lean and history, which are complete.
2 · ChangesThe poll core carries over; the rest is new. v5's walk and epoch-varying biases stay, but at US poll density the walk has to be integrated out by a Kalman filter (v3) and pollster overdispersion partly centred (v4). The race layer (about 100 lines of Stan), fundamentals and seat counts are additions, and the data pipeline is the largest single piece.
3 · RuntimeYes, with v4. The real generic ballot fits in 12 minutes, and a 2026-sized simulated race layer (470 races, 210 pollsters) in 24 minutes, both on a laptop with no divergences. v5's own parameterisation projects to 4–5 hours per chain.
4 · BacktestsIt runs out of sample and matches a corrected poll average; its intervals miss new poll errors. Refitted on each eve from 2018 to 2024 it missed by 2.7 points on average, as the raw poll mean did, and by 2.4 against 3.4 for 2020–2024. Six of eight 90% intervals, on the eve and four weeks out, covered the result. Both misses came in cycles whose poll error grew after the first months.

1 · Can we get enough data?

Every count below I recounted from tables parsed on 2 November 2026, not from an agent's summary. A poll is one pollster's fieldwork ending on one date; its likely-voter and registered-voter versions count once, since the model takes one.

Elections

  • Four poll-era anchors. National House popular vote in 2018, 2020, 2022 and 2024, with FiveThirtyEight's poll archive covering every cycle from November 2016. The model ties its walk to these the way it ties Iceland's to the Alþingi results.
  • 40 House elections since 1946 (20 midterms) for the fundamentals regression. The president's party lost two-party vote share at 19 of the 20 midterms, by 3.8 points on average. That is a strong, simple prior.
  • District and state results 1976–2024 from MEDSL on Harvard Dataverse (CC0). The House file sits behind a one-time guestbook form, which someone has to fill in by hand. The Senate and president files are open.

Pollsters

  • 161 distinct pollsters polled the generic ballot from 2016 to 2026, against 7 houses in Iceland. Only 6 polled in all five cycles; 20–34 carry over from one cycle to the next. That turnover is exactly what v5's epoch-varying house effects are built for.
  • The top ten pollsters take 58–93% of a cycle's generic-ballot polls. YouGov alone ran 73–82 in 2026.
  • Senate polling is a different crowd: of the ≈135 pollsters in 2026's Senate races, only 34 also polled the generic ballot. A state-poll industry bias, separate from the national one, would be a natural extension.

States and districts in 2026

  • Senate: 35 races (33 regular, plus specials in Florida and Ohio). 30 have polls: 15 have 14–78 polls each and 11 have 0–3. The five with none (DE, NJ, OR, WV, WY) are safe seats, where fundamentals carry the race. Four main contests are Republican against an independent (Idaho, Montana, Nebraska, South Dakota), which a three-way D/R/Other party set handles as it stands.
  • House: 435 districts. Nine states (173 districts) vote on new maps after mid-decade redistricting. No full table of 2024 presidential results on the new lines turned up, but Cook's 2026 PVI is published for every district. 35 (2022) and 38 (2024) seats had no major-party opponent; such seats are decided without a model.

Gaps

  • 2026 district polls: Wikipedia lists ≈31. FiftyPlusOne has a full CSV, but it needs an API key.
  • VoteHub's free API stops at 4 October 2026, so the last month comes from Wikipedia alone.
  • Presidential approval: there is no free CSV for the full history. The American Presidency Project has it as HTML tables (Gallup), which scrape easily. Economic series come from FRED without a key.

2 · How much does the model have to change?

The prototype

usa/stan/us_midterm_v0.stan takes v5_epoch as it is and adds about 100 lines of Stan:

  • Election day. One step forward from the last poll, with a fundamentals factor on it, as the forecast model puts its fundamentals prior on the walk's end.
  • Races. Each race is a deviation δr = Xrθ + σracezr from the national walk. Its covariates are presidential lean, the lagged own deviation and incumbency. θ and σrace are learnt from about 1,700 past contested results, entered as data: a district's result of 105 or more votes pins its deviation far tighter than σrace, so making it a parameter would only add stiff directions. A race poll sees the walk plus δr plus its house's bias.
  • Seats. Each race's winner on election day, counted per chamber.

Four revisions followed, each a file of its own with a header that says what changed and why:

  • v1 tightens the priors around US-plausible values: overdispersion near a design effect of 2, industry bias and pollster spread of a few points.
  • v2 makes the walk's step covariance exactly identified. v5 builds it from P scales and a P×P correlation, then centres it onto a (P−1)-dimensional subspace: six numbers for three identified ones when P = 3.
  • v3 integrates the walk out with a Kalman filter (section 3).
  • v4 centres each pollster's overdispersion by its number of polls (section 3).

3 · Does it run in feasible time?

Not with v5's own parameterisation. Two changes that leave the model's structure as it is fix that, and with them the national model fits in about 12 minutes.

What went wrong

Every fit of v5's poll core on US-scale data spent its warmup at the tree-depth cap of 12: 4,095 gradient evaluations per iteration, with step sizes near 10−4. Once Stan had adapted its metric, v2 on the real generic ballot still needed about 800 gradients per iteration, at 10 ms each on a busy machine. That projects to 4–5 hours per chain. Two causes, each diagnosed directly:

  • The walk is pinned by data. About ten generic-ballot polls a week fix each week's level to about ±0.01 on the log-odds scale, tighter than the walk's own weekly step. A non-centred walk is the right choice when the prior dominates; here the data dominate, and the ~990 walk coordinates form long, correlated directions. The Icelandic forecast model met the same thing in a milder form (its sampler-geometry study), where far fewer polls a week left the anchored, partly centred walk adequate.
  • A funnel in overdispersion. Each pollster's log φ sits centred on its hierarchical prior. That suits Iceland's seven houses, each with many polls. Of the 161 US pollsters most have a handful of polls, which say little about their φ, so the joint mode slides to σφ → 0. A Laplace approximation at the mode failed for exactly that reason: its draws put σφ at 0.

The fix

  • v3 integrates the walk out. v5's walk prior is a Gaussian random walk; its anchors and bridges only reparameterise one. Approximate each poll's log-ratio of counts as Gaussian, with the delta-method multinomial variance times the pollster's Dirichlet-multinomial factor (n + φ)/(1 + φ). The walk is then a linear Gaussian state space, and a Kalman filter integrates it out exactly. The sampler sees only the bias, overdispersion, walk-scale and race parameters. Forward filtering and backward sampling draw the walk afterwards, so nothing is lost from the output. A generic-ballot poll has about 500 respondents per party, where the Gaussian approximation is close.
  • v4 centres each pollster's φ by its poll count. The weight is c = s²I/(1 + s²I), with I = polls/8 (the information about log φ per poll when n ≈ φ). This is the rule the forecast model already uses for its constituency effects. A pollster with 1,270 polls is 97% centred; one with 5 polls, 14%.
  • The filter is written in scalars. Stan's autodiff pays a fixed cost for every small matrix operation. Written with 2×2 matrices, the filter took 18 ms per gradient, more than v2's whole model. Written with five scalars it takes about 4.5 ms.

Does v4 give the same answers?

On the small simulated set, v4 and v2 agree, though v2 keeps the exact Dirichlet-multinomial. The election-day vote, the race coefficients, the walk's scales, the pollster spread and the overdispersion all match within a few per cent, and both cover all 18 checked true values with their 90% intervals. They part only on the industry bias of Other against the two big parties. That bias hinges on Other's ~18 respondents per simulated poll, where a Gaussian on the log of a count is crude. US generic-ballot polls report only Democrats and Republicans, at about 500 each. Icelandic polls, with several parties near 3%, should keep the exact likelihood.

The race layer at full scale

The simulator writes a 2026-sized data set: the national walk over four cycles, 210 pollsters, 35 Senate races and 435 districts with 650 race polls, and 1,732 past results for the race regression. v4 fits it in 23.5 minutes, at 8.4 ms per gradient and 31 gradients per iteration, with no divergences and a largest R̂ of 1.012 across every parameter block. Two hyperparameters mix slowest: the spread of pollster overdispersion, σφ, at a bulk ESS of 178, and the drift of pollster bias between epochs, τhouse, at 278. The forecast itself mixes well: the election-day vote, every race's win probability and both seat counts have a bulk ESS of 3,900 or more. A production run would still want longer chains or tighter priors on the two scales.

It recovers 17 of the 18 checked true values within its 90% intervals, about what chance allows (1.8 misses expected). Those include the race coefficients, σrace, the election-day vote and both chambers' seat counts: the Democrats' true 220 House seats against a median of 234 (216–253), and 53 Senate seats against 54 (53–54). The miss is the Senate races' extra share for Other, 0.200 against an interval of 0.205–0.218. This is one simulated data set, generated by the model's own assumptions, so it shows the race layer fits and recovers its truth at full scale. It says nothing yet about whether those assumptions hold for real races.

4 · Does it forecast out of sample?

Each backtest refits v4, unchanged, as if on the eve of an earlier election. It sees only the polls that ended before that election day and the national results of the elections before it, built with the same options as the 2026 fit. A second set cuts the polls four weeks earlier, so the model must also carry its forecast across a month without polls. The forecast is the two-party margin of the national House vote, scored against the national House popular vote as Wikipedia's page for each election gives it.

Two point forecasts from the same polls serve as baselines. The first is the plain mean of the final four weeks' polls, roughly what a poll average shows. The second subtracts the previous election's miss from that mean, a one-line version of what the model's epoch-varying industry bias does. 2018 has no earlier result inside the poll archive's window, and v4 needs one, so its two backtests add the 2016 result on the window's first date; the others leave 2016 out, as the 2026 fit does. With no 2016-cycle polls in the archive, the 2018 backtests have no earlier poll miss to learn a bias from: their industry bias rests on the walk's drift from 2016 and a prior centred on zero. They test the aggregation and the interval more than the bias correction.

Two-party margins in points. An error is forecast minus result, so a positive error is too Democratic; a bold interval misses the result. The fits saw 930–3,683 polls from 40–114 pollsters. The industry bias is the model's estimate, in margin points, of how far that cycle's polls overstate the Democrats; the model subtracts it. Each fit ran 4 chains of 1,000 draws after 1,000 warmup iterations. Every fit had R̂ of 1.012 or less and a bulk ESS of 464 or more on the key parameters and the forecast, and one of the eight (2024, four weeks out) had 1 divergent transition in 4,000. The fits took 0.8–8.2 minutes each, two at a time.

What they show

  • Accuracy: level with the polls over all four elections, ahead once it has history. On election eve the model's median missed by 2.7 points on average, and so did the final four weeks' poll mean. For 2020–2024, when the model had an earlier result to learn a bias from, it missed by 2.4, against 3.4 for the poll mean and 2.5 for the poll mean corrected by the last miss. Four weeks out it missed by 2.2 against the poll mean's 3.4, but the corrected mean did better for 2020–2024 (2.1 against 3.0). With three or four elections a side, differences of a point are within noise: the model's point forecast is about as good as a sensibly corrected poll average, not clearly better.
  • Calibration: 6 of 8 intervals cover the result. A 90% interval should miss about one time in ten, and two or more misses in eight happen 19% of the time by chance alone. Both misses came when the polls missed in a new way: 2020 on the eve, when the polls overstated the Democrats by 4.8 points after an accurate 2018, and 2022 four weeks out. A third, 2020 four weeks out, covered with 0.07 points to spare. All three erred towards the Democrats, the direction of the polls' misses.
  • 2018 shows the cost of little history. With only the 2016 result (R+1.1) behind it and no earlier poll miss, the model split the swing of about 10 points since then between the vote and poll bias, booked +2.2 as bias, and said D+5.0. The polls, at D+8.1, were nearly right. Four weeks out it put the median at D+8.7, with an interval 16 points wide.

Why the 2020 interval missed

The first suspect was the prior on how far industry bias moves between cycles, whose scale (0.05) the backtests share with the 2026 fit. Refitting the four eve backtests with it doubled changed little: the bias carried into 2020 went from +0.1 (−1.5 to +2.0) to +0.2 (−1.6 to +2.1), and the 2020 interval still missed. The pattern points elsewhere. In each backtest from 2020 on, the bias the model carried into the final cycle (+0.1, +0.8 and +3.8) follows the polls' offset from the result in the six months after the previous election (−0.9 after 2018, +0.9 after 2020, +5.3 after 2022), not the miss at the next one (+4.8, +2.7, +2.6). That fits the model's structure. Right after an election the walk is pinned at the result, so the early polls measure the new cycle's bias almost directly, and the model holds that bias fixed for the whole cycle. A miss that builds up later, as 2020's did, goes unseen until the next result. The 2026 forecast carries +2.5, in line with the polls' offset of +2.7 in the six months after 2024.

With one cycle, the 2018 refit's changed prior touches no data, so it is a control. It drew 30 divergent transitions in 4,000 and an R̂ of 1.026, where the original drew none; with 43 pollsters the 2018 fits' spread of overdispersion mixes poorly (bulk ESS 145 and 464). The 2024 refit drew 5 divergent transitions.

Limits

Four elections make a small test. Six covered intervals out of eight cannot tell a well-calibrated 90% interval from an 80% one, and the comparisons with the baselines rest on three or four elections each. The backtests forecast the national vote alone, without fundamentals or races, where most of a full model's value would lie. The priors were set during this study, with these results in the data; none is centred on a Democratic poll bias, but the backtests are not blind to every choice. The 2018 design, with the 2016 result, was settled after the first 2018 fit refused data without any result, before any 2018 forecast existed.

What v4 says about 2026

The real-data fit uses no race data and no fundamentals (a flat election-day factor), so it forecasts only the national House vote. It is a check that the model does something sensible with real polls, not a forecast to publish.

Two-party Democratic margin on 3 November: D+6.5, 90% interval D+3.7 to D+9.6, which is 53.2% of the two-party vote. Polls in the last four weeks average D+8.7 two-party. Five poll averages on Wikipedia, updated 1 November, average D+8.7 on all respondents, D+9.2 two-party.

The gap is the epoch-varying industry bias, the part of v5 the US case leans on. The model estimates that generic-ballot polls overstated the Democratic margin by +0.2 points in the 2018 cycle, +2.6 in 2020, +2.3 in 2022 and +4.1 in 2024. The same polls' means over each election's final four weeks missed by −0.6, +4.8, +2.7 and +2.6 points: near zero in 2018, too Democratic since. The model's figures cover whole two-year cycles, not only their final weeks, so they need not match. It carries an uncertain +2.5 (−0.2 to +4.9) into 2026 and subtracts it. Whether 2026's polls repeat the pattern is tomorrow's question. These numbers are recorded in the repository before the election (usa/README.md).

What a full model would still need

  1. The race data pipeline, the bulk of the work. It needs:
    • 2024 presidential results by state and on the new district maps (Cook's PVI where the maps are new);
    • Senate and House results for 2018–2024, for the race history (MEDSL; the House file needs its guestbook form);
    • candidates and incumbency, and uncontested seats;
    • the special cases: Georgia runoffs, Louisiana's December general election, Alaska's ranked-choice voting, Maine, and the top-two primaries of California and Washington.
    The race layer is tested on simulated data only.
  2. Fundamentals. Presidential approval, real income growth and the midterm penalty, fitted on the 40 House elections since 1946, to replace the flat election-day factor.
  3. Correlated race errors. Races are now independent given the national walk. FiveThirtyEight attributes about a quarter of district uncertainty to regional, demographic and incumbency errors that move many races together. Without such a factor, seat intervals come out too narrow.
  4. A poll bias that can move within a cycle. Both interval misses in the backtests came in cycles whose poll error grew after the first months, which a bias held fixed per cycle cannot see (section 4). A drift on the industry bias within each cycle, or an election-day spread taken from past cycle-to-cycle misses, would widen the forecast where it needs widening.
  5. A separate state-poll industry bias. Of about 135 Senate pollsters in 2026, 34 also polled the generic ballot.
  6. Real anchor sizes. The fits scale each election to 2×105 votes, a concession to v2's sampler. Under the Kalman filter the walk is no longer sampled, so the true ~108 should cost no sampler time; that is untested.
  7. Smaller items. A scalar filter update for polls that report three parties; posterior predictive draws (y_rep), which the house convention asks for; and a score against tomorrow's result.
